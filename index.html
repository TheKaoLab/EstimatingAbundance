Estimating abundance

A 1200 × 800 m plot contains a fixed but unknown number of an endangered oryx species, denoted N. Park managers would like to know how many oryxes there are, but it would be very difficult to count every animal in this space. Instead, we can count a certain fraction of them using different methods, and then try to estimate the total number from that sample. In addition to a number estimate, we want to provide park managers with a range, within which we are fairly confident that the true number of animals lies. The wider the range, the more confident we can be that the true value is within it, but an extremely wide range is not very useful. Conversely, narrower ranges are more precise but less likely to contain the true number.

Which population sizes N are consistent with the data?

A single point estimate cannot be checked against anything: we don't know the real population size, and we don't have access to other population sizes, so there is no ground truth to which we can compare. However, we can use our powers of simulation to generate (simulated) data to get some insight into this question. In this procedure, we assume a certain population size N, then we simulate our survey many times on a population of that size and ask how often our observed result would arise. Candidate population sizes that are unlikely to produce our observed data are rejected, while those that plausibly could generate our observed data are included in our confidence interval. Of course, this process depends on whether our model assumptions match what is really happening in the real world; if they don't, then our results won't be accurate.

The confidence belt

Testing candidates one at a time is laborious, so the procedure is applied across the whole range at once. For each candidate value of N, 400 surveys are simulated and the central 95% of the resulting observations is recorded. The set of candidates whose distribution contains the observed value constitutes our estimate of the confidence interval.

The sampling distribution of the estimator

Here we consider what would happen if we ran the same experiment (with the same population and the same sampling method) many times. Our results would change somewhat each time, even though the underlying system remains the same. This is because there is inherent noise when we sample a portion of the population and extrapolate from that to estimate something about the whole population. Most of the time, our sample will contain a 'typical' set of individuals, but sometimes we might get unlucky and capture an unusually low number of individuals, for example. In this section, we'll look more closely at how random sampling noise can affect our conclusions about the size of the population.

Confidence level

There's no special reason why scientists have decided to calculate 95% confidence intervals (rather than some other probability, lik 95% or 99%). Similarly, there's no special reason that our threshold for significance when doing statistical tests is p = 0.05. These are just arbitrary conventions that we scientists have somehow agreed on. But this choice determines how often we are wrong about our conclusions (i.e., the true value falling within the confidence interval, or determining significance correctly), and represents a trade-off between drawing correct conclusions and the effort needed. If we set our threshold for statistical significance to 0.00001, for example, then statistically significant results would almost certainly be true, but it would take an incredible amount of effort to achieve these results. Physics sometimes has very stringent requirements for significance but also invests a lot in their experiments.

Inverse probability

Sections 3 and 4 both evaluated the probability of observing a particular set of data, assuming that the population was a particular size. This can be written as: P(data | N) (the probability of observing the data given the actual population size). However, this isn't actually what we as ecologists want to know. A more intuitive question to ask is: what is the probability that the actual population is a certain size, given the data that we observed. This can be written as: P(N | data). A worked enumeration

The operation carried out in the above table — multiplying the number of hypothetical plots by the proportion that produced the observed result, then dividing by the total — is Bayes' rule, also called Bayes' theorem. It was set out by Thomas Bayes in a paper published in 1763 and developed independently by Laplace shortly afterwards.

The quantity shown in each column has a name in the world of Bayesian statistics. The first ("Hypothetical plots") is the prior, or the distribution of belief about N held before the data were collected. The second ("How many plots produced the observed result") is the likelihood, the probability of the observed data under each candidate value of N. The third ("Share of survivors") is the posterior, the distribution of belief after the new data have been measured.
Bayes' rule in symbols

Bayes' rule is conventionally stated as a formula rather than as a table. The two are equivalent.

P(N | data)  ∝  P(data | N)  ×  P(N)

The vertical bar is read as "given" or "assuming", and is the only unfamiliar notation involved:

  • P(N), the prior probability of a population size: the first column of the table, and the only quantity not supplied by the data.
  • P(data | N), the likelihood: the probability of the observed data given a population of that size, and the second column of the table. Sections 3 and 4 rest entirely on this quantity.
  • P(N | data), the posterior: the probability of a population size given the observed data, and the final column of the table.
  • ∝, "is proportional to". The product of the first two columns gives counts rather than probabilities; division by the column total converts them, which is the step performed in the last column.

The enumeration above is an instance of this rule rather than an analogy for it. The notation is a shorthand for the counting, and any application of it can properly be asked the same question that the table made explicit: where the first column came from.

Glossary of terms and symbols
Field quantities
N
The size of the population being estimated. It is fixed throughout, and unknown to the observer.
n₁
The number of animals captured, marked and released in the first session of a mark–recapture study.
n₂
The number captured in the second session, whether marked or not.
m₂
The number of marked animals in the second sample. Mark–recapture inference rests on this quantity, which is why studies yielding few recaptures are uninformative.
quadrat
A plot of fixed area searched completely, all individuals within it being counted.
transect
A line traversed by the observer, the perpendicular distance to each detected animal being recorded.
truncation distance (w)
The distance from the line beyond which detections are not recorded. It is set by the observer as a matter of design and is independent of detectability.
detection scale (σ)
The parameter governing the rate at which detection probability declines with distance from the line. Larger values correspond to better detection at range.
σ̂
The circumflex denotes a quantity estimated from data rather than known. σ̂ is the value of σ fitted to the observed distances.
Estimation
estimator
A rule for computing a value from data. Several estimators may be available for the same quantity, and they need not perform equally well.
N̂
An estimate of N, as distinct from N itself.
bias
The difference between the mean of an estimator over repeated sampling and the quantity it is intended to estimate.
CV
Coefficient of variation: the standard deviation expressed as a proportion of the mean, permitting comparison between surveys of different magnitude.
sampling distribution
The distribution of an estimator over repeated application of the same design to the same population. It is displayed in section 4.
standard error
The standard deviation of a sampling distribution.
Chapman's estimator
A modification of the Lincoln–Petersen ratio, due to Chapman (1951), which is very nearly unbiased at small recapture numbers where the uncorrected ratio is not.
Interval estimation
confidence interval
A range obtained by a procedure which, applied repeatedly, contains the true value in a stated proportion of cases. The probability statement attaches to the procedure and not to any particular interval.
confidence level
The proportion of intervals required to contain the true value, conventionally 95%. Higher levels give wider intervals.
coverage
The proportion of intervals that in fact contain the true value. A procedure whose nominal level is 95% but whose coverage is 78% is misstating its own reliability.
confidence belt
The construction of section 3, due to Neyman (1937): the interval comprises those parameter values not rejected by a test at the stated level.
Bayesian inference
prior
The distribution of belief about N held before the data were collected.
likelihood
The probability of the observed data under each candidate value of N.
posterior
The distribution of belief about N after the data have been incorporated, obtained as the product of prior and likelihood, normalised.
credible interval
A range containing a stated proportion of the posterior probability. Unlike a confidence interval, it is a statement about N, and it requires a prior.
Spatial pattern
nearest-neighbour distance
The distance from each individual to the nearest other individual.
Clark–Evans R
The ratio of the observed mean nearest-neighbour distance to that expected under complete spatial randomness (Clark & Evans 1954). Values below 1 indicate aggregation, values near 1 randomness, and values above 1 regular spacing. The expectation used here carries Donnelly's (1978) correction for edge effects.
aggregated
Individuals occurring in patches, as is typical of most species. Aggregation inflates the variance among quadrat counts without biasing the estimate of density.
